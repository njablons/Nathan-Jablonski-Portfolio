## The Arc of This Course

This lab is not a survey of machines. It is a progression of design thinking.

Each technology you encounter this semester has its own design language. What works on a Prusa will not work on a Form 4. What works on a Form 4 will not work with a laser cutter. Learning to design *for the process* — to make decisions about geometry, tolerance, orientation, and material that reflect the specific capabilities and constraints of the machine — is the central skill this lab develops.

**Weeks 1–8: Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM)**
You begin with the Prusa Core One. FDM is the most accessible additive manufacturing technology, and the most forgiving — but designing well for it requires understanding layer adhesion, support structures, print orientation, and how thermoplastic behavior affects your final part. You will move from calibration to functional design, from single parts to assemblies, building toward a complete artifact that demonstrates everything you have learned about FDM design.

**Week 9: Laser Cutting**
The laser cutter introduces a fundamentally different design constraint: you are working in two dimensions to produce three-dimensional function. Joinery, press fits, and interlocking geometry become your primary tools. Parts that would be simple to print must be reconceived entirely for flat sheet material.

**Weeks 10–11: Stereolithography (SLA)**
The Form 4 resin printer produces parts with exceptional surface finish and dimensional accuracy — but it demands a different design approach. Support structures are critical. Resin behavior under load differs from thermoplastic. Tolerances that work in FDM must be recalibrated for SLA. Designing for the Form 4 means designing for precision.

**Weeks 12–15: Final Project**
The semester closes with an open-ended design challenge. You define the problem, select the technology, and see it through to a finished artifact. By this point you will have designed for three distinct processes. The final project asks you to apply that judgment — choosing not just how to design, but which process to design for and why.

Each stage builds on the last. Nothing is wasted.

## Your Machines This Semester

**FDM — Prusa Core One**
Fused Deposition Modeling. Builds parts layer by layer from thermoplastic filament. Fast, accessible, and ideal for functional prototypes. Your primary machine for the first half of the semester. Design considerations: layer orientation, overhang angles, support structures, wall thickness, and infill.

**Laser Cutter**
Precision cutting and engraving of flat sheet material. Design considerations: kerf width, joint clearance, material grain direction, and how two-dimensional geometry creates three-dimensional structure.

**SLA — Formlabs Form 4**
Stereolithography. Uses UV light to cure liquid resin into high-precision parts. Exceptional surface finish and dimensional accuracy. Design considerations: support placement, part orientation, resin shrinkage, and post-cure mechanical properties.

**SLS — Formlabs Fuse 1+** *(selected projects)*
Selective Laser Sintering. Produces strong, functional nylon parts without support structures. Design considerations: powder removal, wall thickness minimums, and how the absence of support structures changes your design freedom.

## How This Portfolio Works

Every lab entry answers three questions about your design process:

**What did the process require?**
Every additive manufacturing technology has design rules — constraints imposed by the machine, the material, and the process itself. Document what those rules were for this lab and how they shaped your design decisions.

**What did you design and why?**
What choices did you make? What geometry, orientation, tolerance, or material decision was specific to the process you were designing for? A good entry shows the reasoning behind the design, not just the finished part.

**What did you learn?**
What worked? What failed? What would you change if you printed it again? The most valuable entries in this portfolio will be the ones where something went wrong and you understood why.

That last question is the standard: could someone read your entry, understand your design decisions, and make better ones because of what you documented?
